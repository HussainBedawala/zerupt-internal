# 04 — The Journal Entry: The Atom of Accounting

## The atom

A **journal entry** is the smallest complete unit of accounting. Every single thing that happens
in the business — a sale, a purchase, a payment, a correction — is recorded as exactly one
journal entry. Nothing is recorded any other way. If you understand the journal entry, you
understand the data model of the entire accounting system.

## Anatomy: a header + two-or-more lines

A journal entry has two parts:

**1. The header** — facts about the whole entry:
- *when* it happened (posting date) and *which fiscal period* it falls in
- *who/what* caused it (source: manual entry, or auto from a sale/purchase/etc.)
- a description, the currency, the exchange rate
- a status (draft → posted → reversed)
- a link back to the source document (e.g., "Invoice #1234") for traceability
- the totals (total debit, total credit)

**2. The lines** — two or more rows, each saying "this account, this side, this amount":
- which **account**
- a **debit** amount *or* a **credit** amount (never both on one line)
- optional detail: party (customer/supplier), tax code, cost center, branch, description

## The rules that make it valid

A journal entry is only allowed to be **posted** if ALL of these hold:

1. **At least two lines.** One line cannot record both ends of a transaction.
2. **Each line is debit XOR credit.** A single line is either a debit or a credit, never both,
   never neither.
3. **It balances: Σ debits = Σ credits.** The iron law from Chapter 01.
4. **Every line points at a valid, active, LEAF account** belonging to this legal entity.
5. **All amounts are non-negative.** You never post a negative debit; you post a credit instead.
6. **The period is open** (not hard-locked).

If any rule fails, the entry must be **rejected before it touches the ledger**. A correct engine
never writes a half-valid entry "to fix later."

## Example: a credit sale with VAT (the realistic case)

You sell goods for 100 + 5% VAT. Customer pays later (on credit).

```
                              Debit    Credit
  Accounts Receivable  105.00            ← customer owes you the full 105 (asset ↑)
  Sales Revenue                  100.00  ← you earned 100 (income ↑)
  VAT Payable                      5.00  ← you owe the tax authority 5 (liability ↑)
                       ──────    ──────
                       105.00    105.00   ✓ balanced
```

Three lines, balanced, each line one-sided, each pointing at a leaf account. This is a textbook
valid entry — and it shows why "just add to one table" (Chapter 01's naive trap) fails: a single
sale legitimately touches three accounts.

## Draft vs Posted vs Reversed (the lifecycle)

- **Draft**: a work-in-progress entry. Fully editable. Not yet part of the official ledger. Does
  not affect balances. May not even have an entry number yet.
- **Posted**: committed to the ledger. Affects balances. **Immutable** — it can never be edited
  or deleted (Chapter 07). Gets a gap-free entry number.
- **Reversed**: a posted entry that has been cancelled by a *second, opposite* entry. The original
  stays; a reversing entry undoes its effect. (We never erase — Chapter 07.)

The only legal transitions are `draft → posted`, `draft → (deleted)`, and `posted → reversed`.

## Manual vs automatic entries

- **Manual** journal entries are written by a human (an accountant booking depreciation, a
  correction, an opening balance). Source = `manual`.
- **Automatic** journal entries are generated by the system when a business event happens — a POS
  sale, a purchase invoice, a stock movement. Source = `auto`. These carry an **event id** so the
  same business event can never produce two entries (Chapter 08, idempotency).

In a healthy retail ERP, the vast majority of entries are automatic. The customer rarely writes a
journal entry by hand — the system does it for them, correctly, every time. That automatic
generation is the **posting engine** (Layer 2).

## The denormalized totals — a subtle point

The header stores `total_debit` and `total_credit` as their own columns, even though you could
re-sum the lines. This is a *performance denormalization* (fast list views without summing
children). The risk: those totals must be kept perfectly in sync with the lines, atomically. If
lines say one thing and the header total says another, you have a corrupt entry. (The schema
audit flagged exactly this as a watch-point — keep it in mind for the code chapter.)
