# 03 — The Cash Flow Statement

## Why the cash flow statement exists

A profitable business can run out of cash. A loss-making business can have growing cash
reserves. Net income and cash movement are different things, and the difference matters
enormously for a retail business.

Here is why they diverge:

- You sell SAR 138,800 of goods. Customers pay SAR 107,600 this month. The remaining
  SAR 31,200 is a trade receivable. Revenue is recognized on the P&L; only the cash
  received hits the bank.
- You buy inventory for SAR 83,400. You pay suppliers SAR 54,650. The remaining SAR 28,750
  is a trade payable. COGS hits the P&L when inventory is sold; the cash outflow happens
  when you pay the supplier.
- You buy shop fittings for SAR 18,000 cash. You put this on the balance sheet as a fixed
  asset and depreciate SAR 1,500 per month. The P&L shows SAR 1,500 depreciation expense;
  the cash outflow is the full SAR 18,000 in the month of purchase.

The cash flow statement reconciles these differences. It shows where cash actually came
from and went to, regardless of when income or expense was recognized on the P&L.

## Two methods: direct and indirect

There are two ways to construct the operating section of a cash flow statement. Both
produce the same final number (cash from operations). They differ in approach.

### Direct method

The direct method shows actual cash receipts and payments:

```
Cash received from customers       XXX,XXX
Cash paid to suppliers            (XXX,XXX)
Cash paid to employees            (XXX,XXX)
Cash paid for rent                 (XX,XXX)
Cash paid for taxes                (XX,XXX)
────────────────────────────────────────────
Net cash from operating activities  XX,XXX
```

The direct method is intuitive. A business owner can immediately see "we collected this
much from customers" and "we paid this much to suppliers."

The problem with the direct method for an ERP: you need to track cash receipts and
payments separately from invoice-level accrual accounting. This requires either a separate
receipts/payments ledger or significant analytical work to extract cash transactions from
the GL. If the system already correctly records AR/AP receipts and payments as GL entries,
this is feasible, but it requires careful account mapping.

### Indirect method

The indirect method starts with net income and works backwards, adjusting for:
1. Non-cash items (depreciation, amortization, FX revaluation)
2. Changes in working capital (receivables, inventory, payables)

```
Net Income                              33,030
Adjustments for non-cash items:
  Add back: Depreciation                1,500
  Add back: FX loss (unrealized)          500
────────────────────────────────────────────
Operating income before working capital 35,030

Changes in working capital:
  Increase in Trade Receivables        (8,200)   ← more credit sales this month
  Decrease in Inventory                 6,400    ← inventory consumed faster than restocked
  Increase in Trade Payables            4,100    ← paid suppliers less than owed
  Increase in VAT Payable               1,200    ← collected more VAT than remitted
────────────────────────────────────────────
Net cash from operating activities     38,530
```

The indirect method is recommended for ERP-derived cash flow statements because:
- All the inputs are already in the GL as account balances.
- Working capital changes are simply the difference between opening and closing balance-
  sheet account balances for the period.
- Non-cash items are easily identified from the account classification (depreciation,
  amortization, FX gain/loss).
- The reconciliation to net income makes the statement self-verifying.

### Why indirect ties to balance-sheet movements

The key insight: a change in any balance-sheet account must be explained by either a cash
flow or a non-cash adjustment to net income. If trade receivables increased by SAR 8,200,
that means the business extended SAR 8,200 more credit than it collected in cash — so
operating cash receipts were SAR 8,200 less than revenue recognized. The indirect method
adjusts for exactly this.

The accounting equation guarantees this: every movement in a non-cash balance-sheet
account has a corresponding impact either on net income (via the P&L) or on another
balance-sheet account (e.g., a loan repayment reduces cash and reduces debt). The indirect
method converts the P&L into a cash view by unwinding all the accrual-to-cash timing
differences.

## The three sections

### Operating activities

Cash generated by or used in the core business operations. This is the most important
section. A healthy business generates positive cash from operations consistently. Negative
operating cash flow sustained over multiple periods is an early warning of distress, even
if the P&L shows profit.

Includes: cash from customers, cash to suppliers, cash to employees, income tax payments,
interest paid (under IFRS, can be classified here or in financing).

### Investing activities

Cash used to acquire or maintain long-term assets, or received from disposing of them.

```
Investing activities:
  Purchase of shop fittings              (18,000)
  Purchase of POS hardware               (4,500)
────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net cash used in investing activities   (22,500)
```

A business that is growing will typically have negative investing cash flow because it is
buying assets. This is fine as long as operating cash flow covers it over time.

### Financing activities

Cash from or returned to funders: borrowing, repaying debt, equity issuance, dividends.

```
Financing activities:
  Proceeds from bank loan                40,000
  Repayment of loan principal            (8,000)
  Dividends paid                              –
────────────────────────────────────────────────
Net cash from financing activities       32,000
```

## The reconciliation to change in cash

The three sections must sum to the actual change in the cash balance between opening and
closing dates:

```
Opening cash balance (1 June 2025)                  68,300
+ Net cash from operating activities                38,530
+ Net cash used in investing activities            (22,500)
+ Net cash from financing activities                32,000
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
= Closing cash balance (30 June 2025)              116,330
```

The closing cash balance must equal the balance of all cash-and-cash-equivalent accounts
(bank, petty cash, short-term deposits) on the balance sheet as at 30 June 2025. If it
does not, the cash flow statement has an error.

This reconciliation is the integrity test of the cash flow statement. Run it. If it does
not close, find the gap before publishing the statement.

## Non-cash transactions

Some significant business events involve no cash movement but affect balance-sheet accounts:

- Converting a short-term liability to equity (debt-to-equity conversion)
- Acquiring an asset in exchange for a liability (finance lease: asset obtained,
  liability recognized simultaneously)
- Writing off a bad debt (receivable reduced, bad-debt expense recognized)

These are disclosed as supplementary information at the foot of the cash flow statement.
They must not be included in the body of the statement since no cash moved.

## Depreciation in the cash flow

Depreciation is the most common non-cash adjustment in the indirect method. It reduces
net income without any cash outflow. The actual cash went out when the asset was purchased
(showing in investing activities). Adding back depreciation in the operating section
corrects for the non-cash nature of the charge.

```
Net income includes depreciation expense:       (1,500)  → reduces net income
Cash flow statement adds back depreciation:     +1,500   → restores to operating cash basis
Investing activities: asset purchase:          (18,000)  → shows the real cash outflow
```

Forgetting to add back depreciation in the indirect method will understate operating cash
flow. Including the asset purchase in operating activities (instead of investing) will
double the error.

## Worked example — summary

Zerupt Demo Retail. Month ended 30 June 2025. Currency: SAR.

```
OPERATING ACTIVITIES (Indirect Method)
Net income                                       33,030
  Add: Depreciation                               1,500
  Add: Unrealized FX loss                           500
                                                ───────
  Operating income before working capital        35,030
  (Increase) in Trade Receivables                (8,200)
  Decrease in Inventory                           6,400
  Increase in Trade Payables                      4,100
  Increase in Output VAT Payable                  1,200
  Increase in Customer Deposits                   4,000
                                                ───────
Net cash from operating activities               42,530

INVESTING ACTIVITIES
  Purchase of Furniture & Fixtures              (18,000)
Net cash used in investing activities           (18,000)

FINANCING ACTIVITIES
  Proceeds from bank loan                        40,000
  Loan repayment                                 (8,000)
Net cash from financing activities               32,000

NET CHANGE IN CASH                               56,530
Opening cash balance                             30,270
CLOSING CASH BALANCE                             86,800

Verification: Bank (84,300) + Cash in Hand (2,500) = 86,800 ✓
```

## The mental model

> The cash flow statement explains why net income differs from the change in cash during
> the period. For an ERP, the indirect method is the right choice: it derives entirely
> from account balances already in the GL — net income from the P&L, working-capital
> movements from opening vs closing balance-sheet account balances. The three sections
> must sum to the actual change in the bank/cash balance, which must match the balance-
> sheet cash accounts at the period-end date. If they do not match, the cash flow
> statement has an error — full stop.
